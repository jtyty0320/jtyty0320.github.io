HRI Systems

Human-to-robot skill transfer, wearable interfaces, and assistive robots

I build systems in which people and robots physically interact: robots that learn touch from people, wearables that read human intention, and robots that assist the human body.

Human-to-robot manipulation skill transfer

Transferring human manipulation skills to robot hands needs more than hand kinematics. The robot also needs to know where contact happens, how strong it is, and how the force is distributed over the fingertip. With PofTac, the same tactile sensor is worn on a human fingertip during demonstration and mounted on a robot fingertip during execution, so the tactile data from both sides are directly comparable (Chung et al., 2026; Chung & Kyung, 2026). This is the topic of my Ph.D. thesis, and ongoing work extends the idea to robot hands with sensing fingernails.

Wearable interfaces for reading human intention

A wearable FMG sleeve with optical fiber sensors recognizes hand gestures and estimates joint angles and grasping force in real time, providing a robust input channel for human-robot interaction (Chung et al., 2025; Chung et al., 2025; Chung et al., 2025).

Wearable assistive and rehabilitation robots

Wearable robots have to follow natural joint motion while staying light enough to wear. For shoulder abduction, we first proposed a four-bar linkage-based support mechanism (Hyeon et al., 2022). I then designed an origami-inspired double-petal mechanism that follows the scapulohumeral rhythm and verified its assistance with surface electromyography (Chung et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2024). A soft wearable robot for elbow flexion and forearm supination/pronation was evaluated with patients with stroke and spinal cord injuries (Jeong et al., 2022).

Prosthetics

A lightweight prosthetic wrist mimics the wrist bones and muscles to reach a functional range of motion (Hyeon et al., 2023), and sEMG signals from the user can be used to control it (Chung et al., 2023).

Wearable thermal feedback

A skin-mountable, stretchable heater array can deliver localized thermal patterns to the skin, which opens a thermal channel for wearable haptic interaction (Kim et al., 2024).
